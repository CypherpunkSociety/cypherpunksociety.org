Personal computing and public cryptography were born in the Bay Area not as instruments of corporate control, but as tools of individual liberation.

04 / 1946–1964 · Steve Blank's "Secret History of Silicon Valley"

Before the Counterculture: Cold War ELINT, Spy Satellites & Stanford

  • Fred Terman, WWII: ran Harvard's Radio Research Lab, 800 engineers, radar jamming and ELINT.
  • 1946: brought the team to Stanford; founded the Stanford Industrial Park.
  • 1953–1964, "Reconnaissance Valley": CIA, NSA, Navy and Air Force money built
    • Sylvania EDL (Mountain View) · ESL (Bill Perry) · Watkins-Johnson
    • Fairchild Semiconductor · Lockheed Sunnyvale, builder of CORONA
  • The reversal: counterculture and cypherpunks turned that silicon and ARPANET against the state.
Frederick Terman (1955): Director of WWII's classified Radio Research Lab and architect of Stanford's Cold War electronics ecosystem.

07 / March 5, 1975 · Menlo Park & SLAC Auditorium

The Homebrew Computer Club

  • From a Menlo Park Garage to SLAC: Started on March 5, 1975 in Gordon French's garage around the Altair 8800 microcomputer, moderated by Community Memory co-founder Lee Felsenstein.
  • The Convivial Design Ethic: Felsenstein applied Ivan Illich's philosophy of "convivial tools" — hardware schematics must be open, understandable, and repairable by the user sitting at a kitchen table.
  • Sharing Code & Schematics: Out of those biweekly meetings at the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center auditorium came the Apple I, Processor Technology, and the open hardware movement.

  • Descendants: 386BSD, then FreeBSD, NetBSD and OpenBSD (OpenSSH).

19 / March 9, 1993 · Eric Hughes

A Cypherpunk's Manifesto

"Privacy is necessary for an open society in the electronic age. Privacy is not secrecy. A private matter is something one doesn't want the whole world to know, but a secret matter is something one doesn't want anybody to know. Privacy is the power to selectively reveal oneself to the world." — Eric Hughes, A Cypherpunk's Manifesto (March 9, 1993)
"We cannot expect governments, corporations, or other large, faceless organizations to grant us privacy out of their beneficence... Cypherpunks write code. We know that someone has to write software to defend privacy, and since we can't get privacy unless we all do, we're going to write it. We publish our code so that our fellow Cypherpunks may practice and play with it. Our code is free for all to use, worldwide."
"Cypherpunks write code": Open-source publication + hands-on public workshops so everyone can verify and run privacy tools.
20 / 1993–1999 · Winning the Crypto Wars with Open Hardware

Sinking the Clipper Chip & EFF's "Deep Crack" DES Cracker (1998)

  • 1993, Clipper Chip: the NSA's MYK-78 with a LEAF key-escrow backdoor.
    • 1994: Matt Blaze forges the 16-bit LEAF checksum
  • 1998, Deep Crack, San Francisco: Gilmore and Paul Kocher, $250,000, 1,536 custom ASICs.
    • A 56-bit DES key in 56 hours; 22 hours with distributed.net (1999)
    • NIST moves to 128/256-bit AES
  • Cracking DES, "Scan this book!": every schematic published as First Amendment speech.
EFF "Deep Crack" Board (1998): 24 custom AWT-4500 DES-cracking ASICs per board (90B keys/sec).
Cracking DES (EFF, 1998): Open-source VHDL chip designs printed on paper to defeat export controls.
MYK-78 Clipper Chip (1993): The NSA key-escrow backdoor chip defeated before Deep Crack shattered 56-bit DES.
