07 / 1978–1984 · Grassroots Dial-Up Networks

The Rise of the Dial-Up BBS & San Francisco's FidoNet

  • 1978 — CBBS (Ward Christensen & Randy Suess): Built during the Great Blizzard of 1978 using a 300-baud modem and Christensen's XMODEM file-transfer protocol — turning any ordinary telephone line into a sovereign, basement-hosted publishing node.
  • 1984 — Tom Jennings Creates FidoNet in San Francisco: Right here in San Francisco, anarchist hacker Tom Jennings built a store-and-forward dial-up mesh that linked 35,000+ independent home BBSs worldwide during late-night off-peak telephone hours.
  • The SysOp & Underground Zine Culture: Tens of thousands of local boards — from phone-phreak hubs and Phrack magazine (1985) to RPG boards like Steve Jackson's Illuminati BBS and the Bay Area boards where PGP 1.0 first spread — formed the true grassroots precursor to the open web.
Hayes 300-Baud Smartmodem (1981): The ATDT command-set modem that powered thousands of home Bulletin Board Systems.
Dial-Up BBS Login Screen: Logging in at 300 or 1200 baud — one phone line, one caller at a time.
08 / June 1983 · Hollywood Meets the Modem

1983: WarGames, Acoustic Couplers & the Birth of the CFAA

"SHALL WE PLAY A GAME?" — WOPR / Joshua in WarGames (1983)
  • The Most Iconic Modem & BBS Film in History: Directed by John Badham, WarGames showed teenager David Lightman using an IMSAI 8080 and a 300-baud acoustic coupler to "war-dial" phone exchanges in search of unreleased computer games.
  • Recruiting a Generation of Hackers: Modem sales surged across America as teenagers plugged family phone handsets into rubber acoustic cups and dialed into local underground BBSs.
  • The Camp David Screening & The CFAA (1984/1986): Five days after WarGames opened, President Reagan screened it at Camp David and asked the Joint Chiefs if it was real — directly leading to NSDD-145 and Congress passing the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), the exact statute later weaponized in the 1990 BBS crackdowns and against Aaron Swartz.
WarGames (1983): Theatrical poster — the film that coined "war-dialing" and popularized dial-up hacking.
Acoustic Coupler Modem: Converting digital bits into audible screeching tones over a standard rotary telephone handset.

10 / 1974–1986 · Tabletop World-Building & Hacker Culture

From Dungeons & Dragons to GURPS: Protocol Thinking on Paper

  • 1974 — Dungeons & Dragons & University Mainframes: Created by Gary Gygax and Dave Arneson, D&D spread through Stanford SAIL, MIT, and UC Berkeley computer labs alongside Unix and ARPANET — directly inspiring Colossal Cave Adventure, Zork, MUDs, and Berkeley's Rogue.
  • 1986 — Steve Jackson's GURPS (Generic Universal RolePlaying System): While D&D was locked into medieval fantasy classes (Fighter, Cleric, Magic-User) and polyhedral dice (d4–d20), GURPS introduced a universal, point-buy simulation engine using three six-sided dice (3d6 Gaussian bell-curve probability) that could model any genre — historical, space opera, or near-future netrunning.
  • Why Cypherpunks Loved Tabletop RPGs: Roleplaying games trained hackers to think in formal state machines, adversarial game masters, and mathematical rulesets — which is why the founding Cypherpunks literally role-played cryptographic protocols with paper slips around a table, and why the 1980s "D&D moral panic" spilled directly into the 1990s hacker crackdowns.
AD&D 1st Edition & DM Notebook: Graph-paper dungeon maps, rulebooks, and polyhedral dice.

11 / March 1, 1990 · Austin, TX & The WELL

The Secret Service Raid on Steve Jackson Games

"Look, this is not science fiction. This is real. It's a handbook for computer crime." — US Secret Service Agent during the March 1, 1990 Raid on Steve Jackson Games
  • Seizing a Tabletop Sci-Fi Game: Armed Secret Service agents raided Steve Jackson Games in Austin, TX to seize the draft manuscript of GURPS Cyberpunk (written by Loyd Blankenship / "The Mentor").
  • Confiscating the Illuminati BBS: Agents seized 3 computers, 300+ floppy disks, laser printers, and the company's dial-up bulletin board system — reading and deleting the private electronic mail of 300+ innocent users without probable cause.
  • The Spark on The WELL & Landmark Victory: When Steve Jackson posted about the raid on The WELL, it galvanized Barlow, Kapor, and Gilmore to found the EFF — leading to victory in Steve Jackson Games, Inc. v. US Secret Service (1993) and Bruce Sterling's classic book The Hacker Crackdown.

13 / Four Blocks from Frontier Tower · 611 Folsom St, SF

611 Folsom Street & NSA Room 641A

  • Right in Our Neighborhood: At 611 Folsom Street (between 2nd and 3rd St in SoMa), AT&T technician Mark Klein discovered that fiber-optic trunks carrying internet traffic across the Bay Area were being split into a secret room: Room 641A.

14 / June 1991 · Boulder & Bay Area BBSs

Phil Zimmermann & Pretty Good Privacy (PGP)

"If privacy is outlawed, only outlaws will have privacy." — Phil Zimmermann, Why I Wrote PGP (1991)
  • Defying Senate Bill 266: Zimmermann put military-grade RSA + IDEA public-key cryptography onto ordinary PCs for human rights activists globally.
  • Cryptography as a Munition: The US Customs Service investigated Zimmermann for 3 years under the Arms Export Control Act (ITAR), which classified strong encryption alongside tanks and missiles.
  • The MIT Press Book Hack (1995): Because exporting software was illegal under ITAR, but exporting a printed book was protected by the First Amendment, MIT Press published the entire PGP C source code as a physical hardcover book that volunteers scanned back in via OCR in Europe.
Phil Zimmermann: Creator of Pretty Good Privacy (PGP), released onto Bay Area bulletin boards in June 1991.

17 / March 9, 1993 · Eric Hughes

A Cypherpunk's Manifesto

"Privacy is necessary for an open society in the electronic age. Privacy is not secrecy. A private matter is something one doesn't want the whole world to know, but a secret matter is something one doesn't want anybody to know. Privacy is the power to selectively reveal oneself to the world." — Eric Hughes, A Cypherpunk's Manifesto (March 9, 1993)
"We cannot expect governments, corporations, or other large, faceless organizations to grant us privacy out of their beneficence... Cypherpunks write code. We know that someone has to write software to defend privacy, and since we can't get privacy unless we all do, we're going to write it. We publish our code so that our fellow Cypherpunks may practice and play with it. Our code is free for all to use, worldwide."
"Cypherpunks write code": Open-source publication + hands-on public workshops so everyone can verify and run privacy tools.
18 / 1993–1999 · The First Crypto Wars

Defeating the NSA's "Clipper Chip"

  • The MYK-78 Clipper Chip (April 1993): Mandated the classified Skipjack cipher with a built-in Law Enforcement Access Field (LEAF) backdoor held in government key escrow.
  • Broken by Science (1994): AT&T Bell Labs cryptographer Matt Blaze discovered that a 16-bit checksum inside the LEAF field could be forged, allowing anyone to use the chip while completely disabling the government wiretap backdoor.
  • Total Victory for Open Cryptography: Combined with EFF's victory in Bernstein v. US and widespread deployment of PGP and SSL/TLS, the US government abandoned the Clipper Chip and lifted export bans in 1999.
MYK-78 Clipper Chip (1993): The NSA key-escrow backdoor chip defeated by cryptographers and civil libertarians.
"Sink Clipper!" Campaign (1993–1994): Grassroots campaign organized by EFF, CPSR, and the Cypherpunks.

28 / Commercial Surveillance & Anti-Fingerprinting

  • The RTB Bidstream Leak: Documented by Johnny Ryan at the Irish Council for Civil Liberties (ICCL) and Wolfie Christl at Cracked Labs — the average user's page loads & location are broadcast hundreds of times a day to data brokers, shaping political micro-targeting and dynamic pricing.
  • Computational Fingerprinting: Even with cookies deleted, your GPU's WebGL rendering, Canvas/SVG font metrics, AudioContext entropy, and modern CSS/hardware side-channels (see research like nullpt.rs) create a unique hardware ID.
  • Live Diagnostic & Defense Stack:
    • EFF Cover Your Tracks (coveryourtracks.eff.org) & The Markup's Blacklight (themarkup.org/blacklight)
    • Tor Browser & Mullvad Browser (uniform letterboxed viewports + identical hardware fingerprint buckets)
Tor Browser (and Mullvad Browser): Defeating computational fingerprinting by making every user's browser look identical.
