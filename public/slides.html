04 / 1946–1964 · Steve Blank's "Secret History of Silicon Valley"

Before the Counterculture: Cold War ELINT, Spy Satellites & Stanford

  • Fred Terman & WWII Electronic Warfare (RRL → Stanford): During WWII, Stanford's Frederick Terman directed 800 engineers at Harvard's classified Radio Research Laboratory (RRL) building radar-jamming and signals-intelligence (ELINT) gear — then brought that team to Stanford in 1946 to launch the Stanford Industrial Park.
  • The CIA, NSA & Reconnaissance Valley (1953–1964): As Steve Blank documented in The Secret History of Silicon Valley, early Valley microwave & silicon firms — Sylvania's Electronic Defense Lab (EDL) in Mountain View, Bill Perry's ESL Inc. ("Electronic Reconnaissance"), Watkins-Johnson, Fairchild Semiconductor, and Lockheed Sunnyvale (builders of the CIA/NRO CORONA spy satellite) — were funded by the intelligence community.
  • The Great Bay Area Reversal: The counterculture and Cypherpunks of the 1970s–1990s took the military-industrial complex's own silicon and ARPANET packet-switching and inverted them into personal computers and public-key cryptography to defend the citizen from the state.
Frederick Terman (1955): Director of WWII's classified Radio Research Lab and architect of Stanford's Cold War electronics ecosystem.
Project CORONA (1960): CIA/NRO reconnaissance satellite built by Lockheed in Sunnyvale — the classified roots of Silicon Valley.

The Homebrew Computer Club

08 / 1977–1984 · Evans Hall, UC Berkeley

UC Berkeley's BSD: Putting TCP/IP & Sockets in the Commons

  • Evans Hall & Cory Hall, UC Berkeley (1977): Graduate student Bill Joy and the Computer Systems Research Group (CSRG) — Marshall Kirk McKusick, Keith Bostic, Michael Karels, and Sam Leffler — turned Unix into an open academic powerhouse.
  • 4.2BSD (1983) — The Internet's DNA: Funded by DARPA, Berkeley engineers wrote the reference implementation of TCP/IP networking and the Berkeley Sockets API (socket(), bind(), connect()), along with vi, csh, and the Fast File System.
  • The Permissive BSD License & 386BSD: By releasing networking code with source and without royalties — and defeating AT&T's USL lawsuit — Berkeley birthed FreeBSD, NetBSD, and OpenBSD (OpenSSH).
Evans Hall, UC Berkeley (1978): Where BSD Unix, vi, and the TCP/IP sockets stack were written.
4.3BSD Manual (1986): Featuring the iconic BSD Daemon ("Beastie").

09 / 1983–1998 · Free Software & Open Source

The GNU Project, Linux & Open Source: Why Privacy Needs Free Code

  • 1983–1989 — Richard Stallman, GNU & Copyleft (GPL): Launching the GNU Project and Free Software Foundation, Stallman used copyright law jujitsu (copyleft) to guarantee the Four Essential Freedoms: to run, study, modify, and redistribute code.
  • The Cypherpunk Bridge — John Gilmore & Cygnus Support (1989): Before co-founding EFF and the Cypherpunks, John Gilmore co-founded Cygnus Support in Sunnyvale (building gcc and gdb) and contributed directly to both GNU and BSD.
  • 1991 Linux & The 1998 Palo Alto "Open Source" Summit: When Linus Torvalds released the Linux kernel (1991) and Netscape open-sourced Mozilla in 1998 (where Christine Peterson coined "Open Source" in Palo Alto), it cemented the cypherpunk theorem: proprietary encryption is a trap; only verifiable open source can be trusted.
Richard Stallman: Founder of the GNU Project (1983), Free Software Foundation (1985), and author of the GNU GPL.
Linus Torvalds: Creator of the Linux kernel (1991) and git (2005).
10 / 1978–1984 · Grassroots Dial-Up Networks

The Rise of the Dial-Up BBS & San Francisco's FidoNet

  • 1978 — CBBS (Ward Christensen & Randy Suess): Built during the Great Blizzard of 1978 using a 300-baud modem and Christensen's XMODEM file-transfer protocol — turning any ordinary telephone line into a sovereign, basement-hosted publishing node.
  • 1984 — Tom Jennings Creates FidoNet in San Francisco: Right here in San Francisco, anarchist hacker Tom Jennings built a store-and-forward dial-up mesh that linked 35,000+ independent home BBSs worldwide during late-night off-peak telephone hours.
  • The SysOp & Underground Zine Culture: Tens of thousands of local boards — from phone-phreak hubs and Phrack magazine (1985) to RPG boards like Steve Jackson's Illuminati BBS and the Bay Area boards where PGP 1.0 first spread — formed the true grassroots precursor to the open web.
Hayes 300-Baud Smartmodem (1981): The ATDT command-set modem that powered thousands of home Bulletin Board Systems.
Dial-Up BBS Login Screen: Logging in at 300 or 1200 baud — one phone line, one caller at a time.
11 / June 1983 · Hollywood Meets the Modem

1983: WarGames, Acoustic Couplers & the Birth of the CFAA

"SHALL WE PLAY A GAME?" — WOPR / Joshua in WarGames (1983)
  • The Most Iconic Modem & BBS Film in History: Directed by John Badham, WarGames showed teenager David Lightman using an IMSAI 8080 and a 300-baud acoustic coupler to "war-dial" phone exchanges in search of unreleased computer games.
  • Recruiting a Generation of Hackers: Modem sales surged across America as teenagers plugged family phone handsets into rubber acoustic cups and dialed into local underground BBSs.
  • The Camp David Screening & The CFAA (1984/1986): Five days after WarGames opened, President Reagan screened it at Camp David and asked the Joint Chiefs if it was real — directly leading to NSDD-145 and Congress passing the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), the exact statute later weaponized in the 1990 BBS crackdowns and against Aaron Swartz.
WarGames (1983): Theatrical poster — the film that coined "war-dialing" and popularized dial-up hacking.

13 / 1974–1986 · Tabletop World-Building & Hacker Culture

From Dungeons & Dragons to GURPS: Protocol Thinking on Paper

  • 1974 — Dungeons & Dragons & University Mainframes: Created by Gary Gygax and Dave Arneson, D&D spread through Stanford SAIL, MIT, and UC Berkeley computer labs alongside Unix and ARPANET — directly inspiring Colossal Cave Adventure, Zork, MUDs, and Berkeley's Rogue.
  • 1986 — Steve Jackson's GURPS (Generic Universal RolePlaying System): While D&D was locked into medieval fantasy classes (Fighter, Cleric, Magic-User) and polyhedral dice (d4–d20), GURPS introduced a universal, point-buy simulation engine using three six-sided dice (3d6 Gaussian bell-curve probability) that could model any genre — historical, space opera, or near-future netrunning.
  • Why Cypherpunks Loved Tabletop RPGs: Roleplaying games trained hackers to think in formal state machines, adversarial game masters, and mathematical rulesets — which is why the founding Cypherpunks literally role-played cryptographic protocols with paper slips around a table, and why the 1980s "D&D moral panic" spilled directly into the 1990s hacker crackdowns.

14 / March 1, 1990 · Austin, TX & The WELL

The Secret Service Raid on Steve Jackson Games

"Look, this is not science fiction. This is real. It's a handbook for computer crime." — US Secret Service Agent during the March 1, 1990 Raid on Steve Jackson Games
  • Seizing a Tabletop Sci-Fi Game: Armed Secret Service agents raided Steve Jackson Games in Austin, TX to seize the draft manuscript of GURPS Cyberpunk (written by Loyd Blankenship / "The Mentor").
  • Confiscating the Illuminati BBS: Agents seized 3 computers, 300+ floppy disks, laser printers, and the company's dial-up bulletin board system — reading and deleting the private electronic mail of 300+ innocent users without probable cause.
  • The Spark on The WELL & Landmark Victory: When Steve Jackson posted about the raid on The WELL, it galvanized Barlow, Kapor, and Gilmore to found the EFF — leading to victory in Steve Jackson Games, Inc. v. US Secret Service (1993) and Bruce Sterling's classic book The Hacker Crackdown.

16 / June 1991 · Boulder & Bay Area BBSs

Phil Zimmermann & Pretty Good Privacy (PGP)

"If privacy is outlawed, only outlaws will have privacy." — Phil Zimmermann, Why I Wrote PGP (1991)
  • Defying Senate Bill 266: Zimmermann put military-grade RSA + IDEA public-key cryptography onto ordinary PCs for human rights activists globally.
  • Cryptography as a Munition: The US Customs Service investigated Zimmermann for 3 years under the Arms Export Control Act (ITAR), which classified strong encryption alongside tanks and missiles.
  • The MIT Press Book Hack (1995): Because exporting software was illegal under ITAR, but exporting a printed book was protected by the First Amendment, MIT Press published the entire PGP C source code as a physical hardcover book that volunteers scanned back in via OCR in Europe.
Phil Zimmermann: Creator of Pretty Good Privacy (PGP), released onto Bay Area bulletin boards in June 1991.

19 / March 9, 1993 · Eric Hughes

A Cypherpunk's Manifesto

"Privacy is necessary for an open society in the electronic age. Privacy is not secrecy. A private matter is something one doesn't want the whole world to know, but a secret matter is something one doesn't want anybody to know. Privacy is the power to selectively reveal oneself to the world." — Eric Hughes, A Cypherpunk's Manifesto (March 9, 1993)
"We cannot expect governments, corporations, or other large, faceless organizations to grant us privacy out of their beneficence... Cypherpunks write code. We know that someone has to write software to defend privacy, and since we can't get privacy unless we all do, we're going to write it. We publish our code so that our fellow Cypherpunks may practice and play with it. Our code is free for all to use, worldwide."
"Cypherpunks write code": Open-source publication + hands-on public workshops so everyone can verify and run privacy tools.
20 / 1993–1999 · Winning the Crypto Wars with Open Hardware

Sinking the Clipper Chip & EFF's "Deep Crack" DES Cracker (1998)

  • 1993–1994 — Sinking the NSA's "Clipper Chip": Weeks after Hughes's Manifesto, the NSA unveiled the MYK-78 Clipper Chip with a built-in Law Enforcement Access Field (LEAF) key-escrow backdoor — broken in 1994 when cryptographer Matt Blaze proved the 16-bit LEAF checksum could be forged.
  • 1998 — EFF's "Deep Crack" Custom ASIC Machine (San Francisco): When the NSA and FBI testified that export-capped 56-bit DES (weakened by the NSA in the 1970s) was "unbreakable," John Gilmore and SF cryptographer Paul Kocher built a $250,000 machine with 1,536 custom AWT-4500 ASIC chips (testing 90 billion keys/sec) — cracking RSA's DES Challenge in 56 hours (July 1998) and 22 hours with distributed.net (Jan 1999), forcing NIST to adopt 128/256-bit AES.
  • Cracking DES ("Scan This Book!"): Every VHDL chip schematic and C program was published in the public-domain book Cracking DES (with the hidden cover subtitle "How federal agencies subvert privacy") to bypass ITAR munitions export bans as First Amendment speech.
EFF "Deep Crack" Board (1998): 24 custom AWT-4500 DES-cracking ASICs per board (90B keys/sec).
Cracking DES (EFF, 1998): Open-source VHDL chip designs printed on paper to defeat export controls.
MYK-78 Clipper Chip (1993): The NSA key-escrow backdoor chip defeated before Deep Crack shattered 56-bit DES.
21 / 2006 · Four Blocks from Frontier Tower · 611 Folsom St, SF

611 Folsom Street & NSA Room 641A

  • The Pivot After Losing the Crypto Wars: Denied mandatory key-escrow backdoors in the 1990s, the NSA turned to tapping the physical fiber-optic backbone four blocks from Frontier Tower at 611 Folsom Street (between 2nd and 3rd St in SoMa).
  • Hepting v. AT&T (2006): AT&T technician Mark Klein discovered fiber trunks being split into secret Room 641A and walked his wiring schematics directly to the EFF here in San Francisco — exposing warrantless backbone surveillance seven years before Edward Snowden.
  • Why End-to-End Encryption Matters: When the physical fiber backbone is tapped at the switching center down the street, only open-source mathematics on the endpoints protects the citizen.

23 / February 2011 – October 1, 2013 · Glen Park Branch Library, 2825 Diamond St, SF

Silk Road & Dread Pirate Roberts: The Market the Manifesto Predicted

  • The Crypto-Anarchist Prediction Comes True (Feb 2011): Ross Ulbricht launches Silk Road as a Tor hidden service with Bitcoin escrow, running it as Dread Pirate Roberts. Adrian Chen's Gawker article (June 1, 2011) makes it the first mass-market darknet.

  • The Cypherpunk Lesson: The cryptography never broke. Opsec, Bitcoin's public ledger, and ordinary police work did the job. A 2024 PoPETs review of 22 US onion-service prosecutions found exactly one attack on Tor itself.

24 / 2013–2026 · From Silk Road 2.0 to Archetyp, Abacus & Telegram

The Darknet Market Era: Every Market Dies One of Two Ways

  • Takedowns (the server was found): Operation Onymous seizes Silk Road 2.0 (Nov 2014) · AlphaBay & Hansa, Operation Bayonet (July 2017: Cazes arrested in Bangkok logged into an unencrypted laptop; Dutch police ran Hansa covertly for a month) · Hydra, 17M accounts (Apr 2022) · Genesis Market, 1.5M stolen browser fingerprints (Apr 2023) · Operation RapTor, 270 arrests (May 2025) · Archetyp, 600K users (June 2025).

  • Where It Stands (2025–26): Chainalysis counts ~$2.6B in darknet-market inflows for 2025, mostly Russian-language Kraken and BlackSprut; half of new markets are Monero-only. The real volume moved to Telegram ($27B through Huione Guarantee before the May 2025 ban) and to ransomware leak sites: a record 7,458 victims posted in 2025.
