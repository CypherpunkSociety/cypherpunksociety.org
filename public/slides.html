Act I · Bay Area Counterculture (1973–1985)

Before the Web: Community Memory in Berkeley

  • August 1973 (Leopold's Records, Berkeley): Lee Felsenstein, Efrem Lipkin, Ken Colstad, and Jude Milhon ("St. Jude") place a noisy Teletype Model 33 terminal in a record store connected by phone line to a mainframe in San Francisco.
  • The World's First Public BBS: Unmoderated, anonymous, walk-up public computing for musicians, activists, poets, and engineers.
  • The Core Ethos: Computers shouldn't just belong to defense contractors and banks—public terminals and open bandwidth are civic commons.
Speaker Note (~3 mins): The cypherpunk story doesn't start in 1992—it starts 20 years earlier in Berkeley with Community Memory. Jude Milhon ("St. Jude"), who helped build Community Memory, is the exact person who later names the Cypherpunks. Notice how our idea of a walk-up Tails OS & Offline Wikipedia station at Frontier Tower is a direct descendant of that 1973 terminal at Leopold's Records.
Act I · Early Net Communities (1985–1990)

The WELL & the Limits of Real-Name Forums

  • 1985 (Sausalito & San Francisco): Stewart Brand and Larry Brilliant launch The WELL (Whole Earth 'Lectronic Link)—the digital town square for Bay Area hackers, journalists, and Deadheads.
  • "You Own Your Own Words": On The WELL, every post was tied to your real legal name and your monthly billing address.
  • 1990 — Operation Sundevil & the Steve Jackson Games Raid: Secret Service raids across the US seize BBS servers and computers, waking hackers up to the reality that centralized servers can be seized overnight.
Speaker Note (~3 mins): The WELL was extraordinary, and it's where John Perry Barlow, John Gilmore, and Mitch Kapor debated civil liberties. But The WELL had an architectural catch: "You own your own words" meant every statement was linked to your credit card and real identity. What if you were a whistleblower, dissident, or researcher who needed mathematical privacy rather than policy promises?
Act I · The Crypto Wars Begin (1990–1991)

The Founding of EFF & Phil Zimmermann's PGP

  • July 1990 — Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF): Founded in San Francisco by John Perry Barlow, John Gilmore, and Mitch Kapor to defend civil liberties in cyberspace.
  • June 1991 — Pretty Good Privacy (PGP): Phil Zimmermann releases PGP 1.0 as free software so ordinary citizens can encrypt email with RSA public-key cryptography—and Kelly Goen uploads it to Usenet and Bay Area dial-up BBSs.
  • Munitions Export (ITAR): The U.S. government investigates Zimmermann for three years for "exporting weapons without a license." Cypherpunks respond by printing PGP source code in hardcover books and on T-shirts as First Amendment speech.
Speaker Note (~3 mins): In 1991, strong cryptography was legally classified alongside artillery and missiles on the US Munitions List. When EFF later represented UC Berkeley grad student Daniel J. Bernstein (Bernstein v. United States), the Ninth Circuit ruled that source code is speech protected by the First Amendment.
Act I · September 1992 (Oakland & San Francisco)

The Birth of the Cypherpunks

  • September 1992: UC Berkeley mathematician Eric Hughes, retired Intel physicist Timothy C. May, and Sun Microsystems pioneer John Gilmore invite ~20 cryptographers and hackers to a meeting at Gilmore's company, Cygnus Support, in Oakland.
  • Coined by "St. Jude" of Mondo 2000: Berkeley writer and programmer Jude Milhon jokes: "You guys aren't crypto-anarchists, you're cypherpunks."
  • cypherpunks@toad.com: Hosted on John Gilmore's personal Sun workstation in his Haight-Ashbury home—growing to thousands of subscribers testing anonymous remailers, digital cash, and mixnets.
Speaker Note (~3 mins): Right across the bridge in Oakland and in Haight-Ashbury just up Market Street from here, Eric Hughes, Tim May, and John Gilmore started meeting monthly. Unlike The WELL, cypherpunks@toad.com allowed anyone to post through experimental Type I anonymous remailers hosted on UC Berkeley's soda.berkeley.edu server.
Act I · March 9, 1993

A Cypherpunk's Manifesto

"Privacy is necessary for an open society in the electronic age. Privacy is not secrecy... We cannot expect governments, corporations, or other large, faceless organizations to grant us privacy out of their beneficence.

Cypherpunks write code. We know that someone has to write software to defend privacy, and since we can't get privacy unless we all do, we're going to write it."
— Eric Hughes (hughes@soda.berkeley.edu), March 9, 1993

Notice the second half of that sentence: "since we can't get privacy unless we all do"—privacy is a collective network effect, not a solo hobby.

Speaker Note (~3 mins): Everyone quotes "Cypherpunks write code," but read the rest of Eric Hughes's sentence: "since we can't get privacy unless we all do, we're going to write it." If only one person uses Tor or encrypted mail, they stick out like a flare. Privacy requires a community teaching, running, and normalizing the tools together.
Act II · Digital Rights & Hacker Lineage

The Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) Today

  • Surveillance Self-Defense (ssd.eff.org): Practical threat-modeling guides for journalists, activists, LGBTQ+ communities, protesters, and international travelers.
  • EFF Open-Source Projects We Will Use:
    • Rayhunter (EFForg/rayhunter): Rust firmware turning a $20 mobile hotspot into a field detector for Stingrays / IMSI catchers.
    • Atlas of Surveillance & DeFlock: Crowdsourced mapping of police ALPR cameras, drones, and facial recognition.
    • Cover Your Tracks: Auditing browser fingerprinting in the wild.
  • Electronic Frontier Alliance (EFA): Grassroots network of local chapters—and tracks like EFFecting Change livestreams.
Speaker Note (~2.5 mins): EFF is headquartered right here in San Francisco on Eddy Street. Their Surveillance Self-Defense curriculum is one of our core teaching pillars, and projects like Rayhunter let us do hands-on hardware workshops detecting cell-site simulators on a $20 hotspot.
Act II · European Counterparts

Chaos Computer Club (CCC) & media.ccc.de

  • Founded in Berlin & Hamburg (1981): Europe's oldest and largest association of hackers, founded by Wau Holland and friends at the table of the Kommune 1 / taz newspaper.
  • Public Demonstrations of Systemic Risk: From the 1984 Bildschirmtext (Btx) bank hack to cloning German interior ministers' fingerprints and auditing electronic voting machines.
  • media.ccc.de Archive: Thousands of world-class lectures from Chaos Communication Congress (38C3, 39C3, ChaosCommunicationCamp) on hardware security, cryptography, and civic resilience that we will screen and discuss.
Speaker Note (~2.5 mins): While the Bay Area birthed the Cypherpunks, Germany's Chaos Computer Club perfected hacker pedagogy and public-interest hardware auditing. Their media.ccc.de archive is an incredible curriculum for our monthly nights.
Act II · The Parisian Bio-Cypherpunk Model

La Paillasse, La Quadrature du Net (π) & Nos Oignons

  • La Paillasse (226 rue Saint-Denis, Paris): An open-science biohackerspace where wet-lab biologists, hardware hackers, and digital rights advocates worked side by side—remarkably similar to Frontier Tower.
  • La Quadrature du Net (π): France's leading digital rights group, famous for the π emblem, the open-source Piphone (VoIP tool for citizens to call lawmakers during surveillance votes), and Autodéfense numérique PGP clinics at La Paillasse.
  • Nos Oignons ("Our Onions"): French community-funded non-profit running high-capacity Tor exit nodes—the sister org to the West Coast's Emerald Onion.
Speaker Note (~2.5 mins): Frontier Tower immediately reminded me of La Paillasse in Paris—where biohackers and digital rights activists from La Quadrature du Net ("Pi") shared the same building and cross-pollinated open science with cryptographic self-defense.

Act III · Threat Intelligence & Spyware Forensics

The Citizen Lab (UofT) & Amnesty Security Lab

  • The Citizen Lab (Munk School, University of Toronto): Led by Ron Deibert; exposed commercial mercenary spyware (NSO Group's Pegasus, Intellexa's Predator, Candiru) targeting journalists, parliamentarians, and human rights defenders.
  • Open-Source Forensics We Can Run Here:
    • Mobile Verification Toolkit (mvt-project/mvt): Open-source CLI tool scanning iOS & Android backups against citizenlab/malware-indicators and AmnestyTech/investigations IoCs.
    • citizenlab/chat-censorship: Reverse-engineering silent keyword & image filtering in messaging apps.
Speaker Note (~3 mins): Citizen Lab doesn't just publish PDFs—they publish open-source indicators of compromise on GitHub that plug directly into the Mobile Verification Toolkit (MVT). That means at Frontier Tower we can teach people how to take a local encrypted backup of their phone and scan it with MVT.
Act III · Measuring Censorship in Real Time

OONI Probe, Citizen Lab Test Lists & Censored Planet

  • citizenlab/test-lists: Community-curated lists of sensitive URLs across 200+ countries.
  • OONI — Open Observatory of Network Interference (ooni.org):
    • Spun out of the Tor Project.
    • Anyone traveling can run the open-source OONI Probe app to measure DNS tampering, TLS middleboxes, and instant-messaging blocks, publishing open data to OONI Explorer.
  • Censored Planet (Univ. of Michigan): Remote global censorship observatory tracking national firewalls alongside OONI.
Speaker Note (~2.5 mins): How do we know when a country blocks Signal, Wikipedia, or independent media? Citizen Lab maintains the open test lists on GitHub, and volunteers around the world run OONI Probe to test them. Updating test-lists or running OONI Probe is something anyone in this room can do in 5 minutes.
Act III · Ad Markets, Fingerprinting & Digital Exhaust

How Your Digital ID Drives What You See Online

  • Computational Fingerprinting: Even without cookies, your browser's Canvas, WebGL, AudioContext, and font list create a unique hardware ID.
    • Live Lab Tools: EFF Cover Your Tracks & The Markup's Blacklight (real-time scanner for canvas fingerprinters, keyloggers, and session recorders).
    • Defense: Mullvad Browser & Tor Browser (identical device profiles).
  • Real-Time Bidding (RTB) Ad Markets (ICCL Enforce & Cracked Labs): Every ad slot broadcasts your device ID, GPS location, and inferred traits to hundreds of brokers—feeding both warrantless government data purchases and micro-targeted persuasion.
Speaker Note (~3 mins): People often think surveillance is just state intelligence agencies. In reality, commercial Real-Time Bidding (RTB) ad auctions broadcast your location and behavioral profile billions of times a day—and intelligence agencies simply buy that data off the shelf, while political operations use it to micro-target what you see.
Act III · Cognitive Security & Algorithmic Auditing

Stanford CSTF, Trust & Safety & Neural Privacy

  • Stanford Cognitive Security Task Force & Cyber Policy Center:
    • Studying AI-driven persuasion, synthetic media, and coordinated influence operations.
    • Open-Source Trust and Safety Curriculum (tsbook.org): Full textbook, slides, and bot-detection labs from Stanford's CS 152.
    • stanfordio/skyfall: Open-source Bluesky AT Protocol firehose tool for studying decentralized social graphs in real time.
  • Algorithmic Auditing: Using Tracking Exposed & AlgorithmWatch to compare how recommendation feeds fragment reality.
  • Neurotech & BCI Privacy: Protecting raw neural/EEG data as brain-computer interfaces move from labs to consumers.
Speaker Note (~3 mins): Especially here at Frontier Tower—where many of us work on neurotech, EEG, and AI—cognitive security isn't abstract. It covers both how generative AI and recommendation algorithms target human cognition, and how we protect raw neural data from becoming the next biometric tracking frontier.
Act IV · Open-Source Intelligence (OSINT)

Bellingcat: Open-Source Tools & Group Challenges

  • Bellingcat Online Investigation Toolkit (bellingcat/toolkit)
  • Open-Source Tools at github.com/bellingcat:
    • auto-archiver: Automatically archives, screenshots, and SHA-256 hashes social media videos/posts before takedowns.
    • osm-search & ShadowFinder: Geolocation and chronolocation from landmarks and sun/shadow angles.
    • sar-interference-tracker: Detecting GPS/radar jamming in Sentinel-1 satellite imagery.
  • Interactive Group Training: challenge.bellingcat.com (100+ OSINT puzzles), Google Colab Research Notebooks, and biweekly Thursday Discord Stage Talks.
Speaker Note (~3 mins): Bellingcat has turned investigative journalism into an open-source software discipline. With challenge.bellingcat.com and their Google Colab Research Notebooks, we can run 90-minute group geolocation and verification nights here at the Tower with zero setup required.
Act IV · Following the Money & Public Records

OCCRP Aleph, ProPublica, MuckRock & Micah Lee

  • OCCRP Aleph (aleph.occrp.org): Open-source investigative data platform indexing global company registries, court filings, sanctions lists, and leaked archives.
  • ProPublica (github.com/propublica): Nonprofit Explorer API, IRSx (990-xml-reader), financial disclosure parsers, and data journalism webinars.
  • MuckRock & DocumentCloud: Open-source FOIA / California Public Records Act (CPRA) request automation.
  • UC Berkeley Human Rights Center (UN Berkeley Protocol) & Micah Lee's Hacks, Leaks, and Revelations (hacksleaks.com / DDoSecrets).
Speaker Note (~3 mins): San Francisco's own Micah Lee (co-founder of Freedom of the Press Foundation and creator of OnionShare) published his entire textbook "Hacks, Leaks, and Revelations" for free online—teaching anyone how to use Python, Docker, SQL, and OCCRP Aleph to investigate real datasets.
Act IV · The Open Knowledge Commons

Wikipedia, Kiwix Offline Archives & Community Fundraiser

  • Wikipedia as Cypherpunk Infrastructure: Headquartered here in SF; fought NSA backbone surveillance in Wikimedia Foundation v. NSA; powers OSINT via Wikidata and CC0 open dumps.
  • Kiwix (kiwix.org) & OpenZIM: Compresses all of Wikipedia, Project Gutenberg, and Stack Exchange into a single .zim file (~50–100 GB) that runs 100% offline on a USB stick or Tails OS drive.
  • Internet-in-a-Box: A $35 Raspberry Pi serving offline Wikipedia & OpenStreetMap over local Wi-Fi during blackouts.
  • Our Goal: Run a Frontier Tower Wikipedia Fundraiser, Edit-a-thon & Kiwix USB Flashing Night.
Speaker Note (~2.5 mins): In an era of AI slop and paywalled platforms, Wikipedia is one of humanity's most important public goods. And through Kiwix and OpenZIM, you can carry the entire encyclopedia offline in your pocket alongside Tails OS.

Act V · What We Will Build Together

Practical Track 3: Freedom Bandwidth Advocacy

  1. Building-Wide Tor Snowflake Proxies:
    • Install the Tor Snowflake browser extension or standalone container across Tower machines.
    • Routes censored users' traffic through ephemeral WebRTC proxies—zero exit-IP liability for shared coworking Wi-Fi.
  2. "100 Mbps for Freedom" Bridge & Relay Pledge:
    • Pool spare cloud VPS credits and isolated VLAN bandwidth to run Tor obfs4 bridges and non-exit middle relays.
  3. Non-Profit Exit Transit Partnerships:
    • Co-host peering & BGP workshops with West Coast non-profit Tor exit provider Emerald Onion (Fremont Cabal IX) and The Calyx Institute.
Speaker Note (~2.5 mins): Privacy tools don't work without bandwidth. Running raw Tor exit nodes on shared coworking Wi-Fi isn't practical, so we use a three-tier playbook: Snowflake proxies on every browser in the building, obfs4 bridges on donated VPS credits, and partnering directly with Emerald Onion for exit capacity.
